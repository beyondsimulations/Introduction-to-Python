Ugly, bad, wrong

Your agent draws, you judge

Ask your agent for a bar chart of revenue per dish:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

dishes = ["Pad Thai", "Miso Ramen",
          "Bao Box", "Falafel Wrap"]
revenue = [420, 450, 330, 300]

plt.figure()      # a fresh canvas
plt.bar(dishes, revenue)
plt.ylabel("Revenue (EUR)")
plt.title("Revenue per dish")
plt.gca()         # show it

Read the code, don’t memorize it. Your job: judge the picture. Here, the names already collide.

Ugly, bad, wrong

Problem charts get one of three verdicts (Wilke 2019, ch. 1):

  • Ugly: clear and correct, but unpleasant to look at
  • Bad: the numbers are right, but the picture is unclear, confusing, overloaded or deceiving
  • Wrong: the numbers in the picture are not the data

Fix wrong first, then bad. Ugly comes last: a correct, clear chart in default colors already does its job.

Predict: which chart is wrong?

Three drafts from Tobi’s agent. Exactly one is wrong:

a) chart 1
b) chart 2
c) chart 3

Answer: chart 1 adds up to 115 %

a) chart 1: shares of one whole add up to 100 %, and these add up to 115 %. That’s wrong. Chart 2 is ugly: loud, but you can read every number. Chart 3 is bad for this question: correct, but nobody can tell which slice is biggest.

The title is a claim

A title states the point (Wilke 2019, ch. 22):

Describes the chart States the point
Revenue per dish Miso Ramen sells best: 450 euros
Delivery times Most deliveries take 20-30 minutes
Order value vs. delivery time Big orders don’t take longer
  • Write the claim before you draw. If you can’t, the chart has no point yet.
  • Every axis names what it measures, with the unit: Revenue (EUR).

Match the chart to the question

The question The basic chart
How much per category? bar, sorted
How are values spread? histogram
What share of the whole? pie, for halves and quarters
Do two numbers move together? scatter
How does it change over time? line
Where, and how sure? map, error bars

A quick tour of each family (Wilke 2019, ch. 5):

Amounts: how much?

Bars start at 0, a dot may zoom: the next block says why.

Distributions: how spread out?

Also in the directory: cumulative densities, q-q and sina plots.

Proportions: what share?

Also in the directory: treemaps, parallel sets, nested pies.

Relationships: two numbers

A shapeless cloud is a finding too: it goes in the title like any claim.

Time series: change over time

Many lines? One accent, the rest grey. More after the break.

Maps and uncertainty

Also in the directory: cartograms, eyes plots, quantile dots.

Predict: how many bars?

The same four numbers, two ways. On the left, a bar per number; on the right, a histogram:

values = [8, 12, 9, 15]
plt.bar(range(4), values)   # left
plt.hist(values, bins=3)    # right

The bar chart shows 4 bars. How many bars does the histogram show?

a) 4: one bar per number
b) 15: one bar per unit up to the max
c) 3: one bar per range

Answer: three ranges, three bars

c) 3: 8 and 9 share the first range, so that bar is 2 tall. A bar chart shows one bar per category, as tall as its value. A histogram shows one bar per range, as tall as the number of values in it.

Proportional ink

Predict: how many times more?

Miso Ramen vs. Falafel Wrap: how many times as much?

a) about 1.5 times
b) about 3 times
c) about 6 times

Answer: 1.5 times, drawn as 6

a) about 1.5 times: 450 against 300. Cut at 270, the bars keep 180 and 30 of their length, so the picture says 6 times. That’s bad: the numbers are right, the picture misleads. The rule behind it is proportional ink: the ink for a value has to grow with the value (Wilke 2019, ch. 17).

Predict: the AI’s one extra line

Revenue [710, 718, 724, 731], up 3 % over four weeks. Tobi’s AI drafts the chart and adds one line:

plt.plot([1, 2, 3, 4], [710, 718, 724, 731])
plt.ylim(700, 735)

What does the room see?

a) a flat line: 3 % stays 3 % on any axis
b) an error: ylim has to include zero on a line chart
c) a line rising across most of the chart

Answer: a climb across the chart

c) a line rising across most of the chart: the axis spans 35 units and the line rises 21 of them, 60 % of the picture for 3 % of growth. Python raises no error.

Bars from zero, dots may zoom

  • A bar shows its number by its length: cut the axis and the comparison breaks. Bars always start at 0.
  • A dot shows its number by position only, so a dot plot may zoom (Wilke 2019, ch. 17).
  • A line sits in between: zooming blows a small change up. Plot the change from 0, or put its size in the title: “+3 % in four weeks”.

The investor reads the axis before the line, and then the title, to see if the two agree.

Make one point

Makeover 1: who is fastest?

“Are you faster than the rivals?” First: who was fastest in week 1?

a) MunchCorp
b) Kiezküche
c) Pronto

Answer: Kiezküche, the slow way

b) Kiezküche: 30 minutes in week 1. An easy number, found the slow way: you matched four colors to a legend sorted from A to Z. And her real question, whether we got faster, is buried even deeper.

The data is fine. The chart is bad: the point is in there, buried. Four changes dig it out, one at a time, and none of them touches the numbers.

Lines, step 1: one accent color

Take the color out of everything except the point (Wilke 2019, ch. 4). One color= per line, and the eye knows where to go.

Lines, step 2: labels, no legend

Names at the lines replace the legend, and they work for the 8 % of men who can’t tell red from green (Wilke 2019, ch. 19).

Lines, step 3: the title says it

The title states the claim; frame and tick marks are gone. Same numbers, and every change was a decision.

Lines, step 4: add the context

The rivals only matter as a yardstick: one dashed line for the fastest rival replaces three, and one note marks week 3. Now the chart tells a story.

Lines, step 5: one too many

Every number, line and box here is true, and none of them helps: the point drowns. Balance data and context (Wilke 2019, ch. 23): stop at step 4.

Makeover 2: what misleads?

Which problem makes the investor believe something false?

a) the loud colors
b) the axis from 5 %
c) the tilted names

Bars, step 1: from zero

b) the axis. Cut at 5 %, Harburg looked five times as late as Eppendorf; from 0 it is three times. The colors and the tilt only make the chart harder to read. Fix what misleads first.

Bars, step 2: sorted, lying down

Sorted, the ranking reads from top to bottom. Lying down, the names need no tilt. The colors meant nothing, so one color is enough, and the legend goes with them.

Bars, step 3: one point

One accent for the point, the numbers on the bars instead of an axis, the claim on top. Step 1 fixed what misleads, step 2 what confuses, step 3 makes the point.

Makeover 3: who is late?

We promise 35 minutes. Which zone breaks it most often?

a) Altstadt, orange
b) Hafen, green
c) Nord, blue

Spread, step 1: one panel each

b) Hafen: three of four deliveries take longer than 35 minutes. One panel per zone shows it, and shows what the overlap hid: Altstadt has two humps, a fast group and a slow one.

Spread, step 2: boxes and dots

One chart instead of four: a box per zone for median and spread, every delivery as a dot beside it, from the most punctual zone to the least.

Spread, step 3: draw the promise

A reference line turns the chart into a test: every dot above it is a broken promise. The share on top and the title give the number.

Charts to a story

A deck is a story

A story is facts in a specific order that create a reaction (Wilke 2019, ch. 29). A pitch in four steps:

  1. Opening: where we stand
  2. Challenge: the problem
  3. Action: what we did about it
  4. Resolution: the result, and what we ask for

Each step is one slide with one point, a figure for the generals: the room gets it in seconds.

Predict: Tobi’s four slides

Opening, challenge, action, resolution: in which order?

a) B, D, C, A
b) A, B, D, C
c) B, C, D, A

Answer: B, D, C, A

a) B, D, C, A: a growing business (B), the slowest in town (D), new routes and a line that falls below every rival (C), customers who come back (A). Then the ask: one more rider keeps it that way.

Option b) leads with the result, an arc that works too, in a newspaper. In a pitch, the problem first makes the result count.

Two rules for the whole deck

  • Build up. Show our line alone before you add the rivals. A complex chart lands once the room has seen its simple version.
  • Keep colors fixed. Our line is dark red on every slide. Paint us blue on slide 3 and the room reads someone else’s line as yours.

Consistent, not repetitive: same colors and same fonts across the deck, but each slide a different kind of chart, so the room notices that something new is coming.

AI as your chart assistant

  1. Describe data and point: “Revenue per zone from orders (zone, total_eur), sorted bars, top zone dark red, rest grey, a title that names the leader.”
  2. Let it draft.
  3. Verify before you believe the picture:
    • the columns exist · the bars match your own groupby
    • bars from 0 · the chart type fits · the title says no more than the picture

AI drafts. You decide what goes on the slide.

Three ways AI charts mislead

  • Invented column names. It writes orders["revenue"], but your column is total_eur: the same confident nonsense you caught in Episode 8.
  • Defaults nobody chose. Bars in alphabetical order, one color per category, a legend, an axis cut to fit the data. Each one is a decision the agent made silently.
  • Your words as the title. Ask for a chart “showing that sales are growing” and that’s the title you get, whatever the line does.

For all three: check the numbers, the axis and the title before a chart goes on a slide.

Wrap-up

Three things to remember

  1. Judge every chart. Wrong first (shares that add up to 115 %), then bad (the pie), ugly last. Bars start at 0; a zoomed line needs a title with the real size.
  2. The question picks the chart, the title states the claim. Amounts → bar, spread → histogram, two numbers → scatter, time → line.
  3. One point per chart, one story per deck. One accent color, names on the lines, and the slides in an order that ends in your ask.
